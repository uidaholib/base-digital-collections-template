Heated arguments about this matter are seen in the tents, at the latrines and the space between the water sink area and the mess hall. The most unbearable thing to overhear is indirectly insulting statements made by those who answered yes aimed at those who answered no, and also those who seem to be well informed secretly trying to convince as many people as possible to give the same answer yes as they did. They are telling others that all internees in this camp are doomed to be exchanged in the future, and there is no use to stick to the U.S. any longer. It must be truly difficult to leave the wife, children and property behind and abandon the residence for the last half of the life. At the same time, I don't think there are a lot of married couples with such strong trust built between them that the husband can go ahead and go back to the homeland by himself upon the wife and children's approval. Even if someone does not have anything to be concerned about what to do with the property or what will happen in the future, there are few families among the fellow Japanese living in the U.S. where the husband can make everyone follow his unilateral order. 

They are dreaming of rebuilding their life after the war and cannot abandon the enemy nation despite the past violent war between Japan and the U.S. because they have raised their children as a U.S. citizen in their families. I cannot help feeling sympathetic toward them.
