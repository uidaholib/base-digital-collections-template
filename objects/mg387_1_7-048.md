The range of mountain peaks surrounding the basin was brushed with rain clouds, and it somehow conjured up some feelings.

There were houses alongside shortly after the train departed, and I saw men and women strangely looking at us. Two young men spread their fingers in front of their nose in an insulting manner, while a young couple, probably husband and wife, were on the porch of their poor house. The woman raised her hand and innocently waved it bye bye, and the man suddenly grabbed her hand and put it down. It was relatively a remote region, and it seems like those people lack interest in the war along with hostility or are sympathetic toward non-combatting civilian residents of the U.S. Such an occasion gives us a glimpse of humanity. 

The windows were surprisingly not covered with lowered shades, and it was good that we were freely able to see outside. 

At night, a seemingly non-commissioned officer showed us how to assemble make-shift beds. It was inserting and sandwiching one back piece between two seats to make a bed big enough for two people to lie down side by side. I have been in the U.S. for 30 years, and I heard of this method for the first time. I would say this is a goodwill gesture for us given by the military. 

We departed, through a forest of firearms, loaded with bullets by a line of soldiers

Clouds arise on mountain peaks in Missoula, in the morning we left with fighting cheers of banzai

I am crying over farewell as I sent away, as a victim of a war
